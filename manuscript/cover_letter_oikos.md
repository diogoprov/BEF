Dear Editor,

Please find attached our manuscript entitled "Whole-community phylogenetic diversity restates trophic structure in experimental pond food webs", which we submit for consideration as a Research Paper in Oikos.

Phylogenetic diversity has been proposed as a summary of community composition that predicts ecosystem functioning from single trophic levels to complex food webs. However, this idea has rarely been tested in experiments that manipulate more than one trophic level. In this manuscript, we reanalyse two pond mesocosm experiments in which the richness and composition of macrophytes, grazers and invertebrate predators were manipulated simultaneously, and in which productivity, respiration and decomposition were measured repeatedly before and after an acid pulse. We show that, in these food webs, richness and functional-group composition accounted for 89–98% of the among-composition variance in whole-community phylogenetic diversity, that no phylogenetic metric affected ecosystem processes at a fixed trophic structure, and that explicit functional-group composition outperformed any single metric across trophic configurations. Additionally, we show with simulations that compositions drawn from the same species pool are statistically dependent, so that analyses treating them as independent units inflate false positives, and we identify the features that an experiment would need to separate relatedness from trophic structure and species identity.

We believe that Oikos is the right outlet for this work for three reasons. Firstly, the manuscript addresses a general conceptual question, namely whether evolutionary history adds information about ecosystem functioning beyond richness and composition, and frames it within the Community Assembly and the Functioning of Ecosystems perspective. Secondly, its conclusions apply to any multitrophic system in which phylogenetic metrics are computed across trophic levels, beyond the ponds studied here. Finally, the two boxes, one on phylogenetically structured species effects and one on experimental design, offer practical guidance for future biodiversity experiments, in line with the journal's interest in work that generates insights across taxa and ecosystems.

The experiments were originally published by Downing and Leibold (2002, Nature) and Downing (2005, Ecology), which analysed the effects of richness and composition but not of phylogenetic diversity, and by Downing and Leibold (2010, Freshwater Biology), which analysed resistance and resilience to the acid pulse. None of these studies used phylogenetic information. An earlier version of these analyses was part of the PhD thesis of the first author (2016), which was not published in a peer-reviewed outlet; the present analyses replace it entirely, because we found and corrected errors in the derived data used at that time. The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, and all authors have approved its submission. We declare no conflict of interest.

The data, phylogenies and code needed to reproduce all analyses are provided for review through an anonymized copy of the project repository [URL anonymous.4open.science A GERAR] and will be archived in Zenodo with a DOI upon acceptance.

# Significance Statement (submission form)

Phylogenetic diversity has been proposed to predict ecosystem functioning from single trophic levels to complex food webs, but this idea has not been tested in experiments manipulating several trophic levels at once. Using two pond mesocosm experiments with manipulated macrophytes, grazers and predators, we show that whole-community phylogenetic diversity largely restates trophic structure and does not add information about productivity, respiration or decomposition. This conceptual result applies to any study computing phylogenetic metrics across trophic levels, which fits the broad, synthetic scope of Oikos. The work builds on the original experiments of Downing and Leibold (2002, 2010) and Downing (2005), which established the effects of richness and composition in these food webs, and on the Community Assembly and the Functioning of Ecosystems perspective (Leibold et al. 2017). It extends them by asking whether evolutionary relatedness explains composition effects, by showing that compositions sharing species are statistically dependent, and by proposing designs that can separate relatedness from species identity.
