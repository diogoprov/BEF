# Discussion

We found that, in two experiments that manipulated macrophytes, grazers and invertebrate predators simultaneously, phylogenetic diversity computed over the whole community was almost entirely determined by species richness and by the functional groups present, and that it did not affect productivity, respiration or decomposition once the experimental design was accounted for. Additionally, across trophic configurations, explicit functional-group composition explained more of the among-composition variance in productivity and respiration (91% and 80%) than the best single phylogenetic metric (VPD; 72% and 68%), whereas PD explained the least (13–14%). To our knowledge, this is the first test of the hypothesis that phylogenetic diversity predicts ecosystem functioning in experimental freshwater food webs in which three trophic levels were manipulated at the same time. Our simulations further showed that neither these designs nor most realistic alternatives can separate an effect of relatedness from the identity of the species that generate it. Taken together, these results suggest that, in multitrophic communities, whole-community phylogenetic diversity seems to restate trophic structure rather than add information about it, and that the question of whether evolutionary history matters for ecosystem functioning in food webs remains open.

Srivastava et al. (2012) argued that phylogenetic diversity may be useful to predict ecosystem functions in communities ranging from single trophic levels to complex networks, because many traits show phylogenetic signal and because phylogeny also shapes interactions among species. Accordingly, when we started this work, we reasoned that phylogeny could be a better proxy than measured traits in communities composed of distantly related organisms, because closely related species within a functional group would share unmeasured effect traits. However, a metric computed over the whole community does the opposite, since its value is dominated by the distances between functional groups rather than within them. In our experiments, the deepest splits of the tree separated plants from animals and vertebrates from protostomes, so that PD and MPD mostly counted how many trophic groups a composition contained and which ones. Indeed, only 4% of the variance of PD and 7% of the variance of MPD remained within richness levels in T98, and less than 3% of the variance of any metric remained within design cells in T99. Interestingly, the only metric that retained substantial variation in T98 (VPD, 89%) was also the one that best summarized trophic configuration across the cells of T99, which seems to reflect that the variance of pairwise distances changes sharply when plants and animals are combined. Similarly, PD explained the least of the among-composition variance in T99, probably because 74% of its own variance was already explained by species richness, which was included in all models. Thus, the metrics behaved as coarse descriptors of the design, and their apparent explanatory power across trophic configurations was borrowed from functional-group composition.

This pattern adds a trophic dimension to a more general problem, namely that phylogenetic diversity is an unreliable surrogate for functional diversity. Phylogenetic diversity is expected to track multivariate trait diversity when many traits evolve under similar models (Tucker et al. 2018), but this relationship weakens when rates and optima of trait evolution vary, and maximizing phylogenetic diversity captures more functional diversity than random choice in only part of the comparisons (Mazel et al. 2018). In multitrophic communities, the problem is compounded because the traits that determine how a species affects an ecosystem process differ among trophic levels: the traits that matter for a macrophyte (e.g., growth form) are not comparable with those that matter for a grazer or a predator. Consequently, a distance between a snail and a pondweed has no obvious functional meaning, although it dominates the value of the metric. In contrast, Thompson et al. (2015) computed phylogenetic diversity within a single trophic level (zooplankton) and used it to predict a function at another level (phytoplankton abundance), which keeps the metric interpretable. Therefore, we suggest that studies relating phylogenetic diversity to ecosystem functioning in food webs should compute metrics within trophic levels, and should first quantify how much of their variance is independent of the experimental design, as we did here.

Phylogenetic diversity did not affect any response at a fixed richness or design cell, in either experiment, for stocked or realized compositions. However, these null results should be interpreted as a lack of information rather than as evidence that relatedness does not matter, because the design left very little variation in the metrics to estimate their effects. In T99, for instance, a slope had to be estimated from 1–3% of the variance of the predictor, distributed among 26 compositions. Moreover, the variation that remained within functional groups followed a few deep splits that coincided with recognizable taxa, so that MPD within groups mostly indexed whether a composition contained tadpoles, snails or beetles. As a consequence, it is difficult to assign any effect of within-group relatedness to evolutionary history rather than to the identity of a single species, such as *Lithobates catesbeianus*, whose tadpoles are large grazers. Our model with phylogenetically structured species effects confirmed this limitation, because the posterior of the phylogenetic share was practically identical to its prior (Box 1).

One important limitation of our study is therefore the design itself, which was built to test richness and composition rather than relatedness (Downing and Leibold 2002, Downing 2005). Experiments in grasslands faced a related problem, as phylogenetic diversity and species richness are strongly correlated when species are drawn at random, and reanalyses of 16 grassland experiments found that phylogenetic diversity did not affect biomass or its stability after controlling for richness (Venail et al. 2015). Nonetheless, that conclusion was contested, and alternative analyses gave contradictory or inconclusive support for phylogenetic diversity over richness (Cardinale et al. 2015). Contrarily to grasslands, where the debate concerns the correlation between two diversity measures within one trophic level, in our experiments phylogenetic diversity was confounded with trophic structure and, within trophic levels, with species identity. Thus, our results do not contradict experiments that found positive effects of phylogenetic diversity on productivity at a single trophic level (Flynn et al. 2011, Cadotte 2013, Venail and Vives 2013), but show that the same inference cannot be drawn from multitrophic designs with random assembly.

Even with a design suited to the question, there are reasons to expect a weak link between the phylogenetic diversity of the manipulated species and the processes measured in these mesocosms. Firstly, productivity and respiration were estimated from diel oxygen dynamics, to which phytoplankton contributed an order of magnitude more than macrophytes and periphyton (Downing and Leibold 2002, 2010). As a result, the manipulated species seem to have affected these processes mostly indirectly, through trophic cascades, grazing on periphyton and nutrient recycling, and their relatedness is at least two links removed from the organisms that generate the signal. Theory predicts that food-web structure can change not only the strength but also the direction and shape of biodiversity–ecosystem functioning relationships relative to single trophic levels (Thébault and Loreau 2003, Duffy et al. 2007), which further weakens any expectation of a simple relationship between relatedness and function. Secondly, the grazer guild combined tadpoles, snails, amphipods and corixids, which are among the most distantly related species of the pool but share a similar trophic role. This functional convergence across deep lineages decouples relatedness from effect traits, which is the condition under which phylogenetic diversity stops predicting functioning (Srivastava et al. 2012). For instance, in bacterial communities, phylogenetic diversity predicted productivity among ancestral lineages, in which productivity was phylogenetically conserved, but not among lineages that had evolved on different resources (Gravel et al. 2012). Similarly, when feeding-trait diversity and phylogenetic diversity of grazing amphipods were manipulated independently in mesocosms, only trait diversity affected coexistence and food availability, probably because the traits that govern resource use in animals are evolutionarily labile (Best et al. 2013). In the same vein, body size predicted the top-down control of algae by zooplankton, whereas phylogenetic distance explained none of the variation in grazing rates (Gianuca et al. 2016). Unfortunately, we do not have trait data for the species of these experiments to test whether effect traits are phylogenetically conserved within each functional group. Future studies should measure effect traits (e.g., grazing rate, body size and excretion rate) together with the phylogeny, and model species effects with both trait and phylogenetic components (Ives and Helmus 2011), to test whether relatedness adds information beyond traits.

The acid pulse reduced respiration by about one standard deviation in both experiments, and respiration recovered faster in the richest compositions of T98, which agrees with the higher resilience of respiration in species-rich communities reported by Downing and Leibold (2010). However, phylogenetic diversity did not robustly modulate resistance or recovery. In T99, an apparent negative effect of MPD on the recovery of productivity and respiration depended on a single composition, in which the tanks assigned to the pulse already differed from the controls before the pulse. Therefore, we do not interpret this effect, and the episode illustrates how a single unbalanced composition can produce a credible interaction term when compositions are few. On the other hand, higher phylogenetic diversity was associated with a larger increase in decomposition after the pulse in T99, and this effect was robust to leaving out single compositions. One possible explanation is that the grazer guild included snails that also feed on decomposing leaves, as *Physa* spp. and *Helisoma trivolvis* did when offered maple leaves in the laboratory (Lombardo and Cooke 2002), and detritivore diversity has stronger effects on decomposition than detrital diversity (Srivastava et al. 2009). However, the leaves were enclosed in mosquito netting, which may have excluded most macroinvertebrates, so that microbial decomposers seem a more likely route. Nonetheless, this effect was not found in T98, it was one of 48 modulation terms estimated across experiments, and we cannot rule out that it arose by chance. Contrarily to grassland experiments in which phylogenetic diversity increased the temporal stability of biomass (Cadotte et al. 2012), our results provide no evidence that relatedness among species of different trophic levels stabilizes ecosystem processes after a pulse disturbance.

Our simulations revealed a problem that extends beyond phylogenetic diversity. Compositions drawn from the same species pool share species, so that their responses are correlated through the effects of those species. Treating compositions as independent units, as is common when composition enters a mixed model as an independent random effect (Hector et al. 2011, Schmid et al. 2017), inflated false positives to 10–25% with 24 compositions and to 16–52% with 96, because adding compositions from a fixed pool adds little independent information. This agrees with simulations in which naive regressions across communities that overlap in species and phylogeny had type I error rates of 15.8–28.6% (Tsang and Cadotte 2025). Thus, the models of our own experiments, with 21 and 26 compositions, were in the range in which a nominal 5% error rate becomes about 10–25%, which reinforces our cautious reading of the few effects whose intervals excluded zero. In contrast, modelling shared species with covariance matrices derived from species incidence and phylogeny restored error rates close to nominal when the uncertainty of the variance components was propagated (Box 2). Therefore, we suggest that analyses of biodiversity experiments that test composition-level predictors should model species overlap explicitly, as in Diversity–Interactions models (Kirwan et al. 2009, Connolly et al. 2011) or in phylogenetic mixed models (Ives and Helmus 2011).

Experiments that manipulate diversity across more than two trophic levels remain rare. For instance, among 94 diversity manipulations compiled by Lefcheck et al. (2015), 47 manipulated plants, 16 herbivores and only three carnivores, each manipulating a single group, and the consequences of phylogenetic diversity for functioning in multitrophic systems are still poorly studied (Eisenhauer et al. 2019). Indeed, the experiments that brought phylogeny into multitrophic settings manipulated it at a single trophic level and followed its consequences upwards; for instance, tropical tree plots with high phylogenetic diversity changed plant defences, herbivore damage and predator activity (Alavez et al. 2023), and tree phylogenetic diversity was related to the composition of arthropod and fungal communities across trophic levels in a subtropical forest (Staab et al. 2021). Our simulations indicate that a test of relatedness in food webs requires three features (Box 2): (i) a large species pool within the focal trophic level, so that relatedness can vary without a few deeply divergent taxa carrying the gradient; (ii) compositions selected along relatedness with balanced species incidence, following the logic of designs that separate identity from richness (Bell et al. 2009, Cadotte 2013); and (iii) analyses that model shared species. Concurrently, frameworks that treat trophic structure explicitly, such as energy flux through food webs (Barnes et al. 2018) or the vertical diversity hypothesis (Wang and Brose 2018), seem better suited than whole-community metrics to describe how diversity across trophic levels affects ecosystem processes. In these frameworks, phylogenetic diversity would enter within trophic levels, as a description of the species that perform each function, rather than as a summary of the whole web.

In summary, we have shown that, in experimental pond food webs, whole-community phylogenetic diversity largely re-describes trophic structure, that it did not predict ecosystem functioning at a fixed trophic configuration, and that random assembly from small species pools cannot separate relatedness from species identity. Our results do not reject the hypothesis that evolutionary history matters for ecosystem functioning in food webs; rather, they show what the designs available so far can and cannot say about it. Future studies should manipulate relatedness within one trophic level while holding the rest of the food web constant, measure effect traits along with the phylogeny, and model the non-independence that shared species create among compositions.
