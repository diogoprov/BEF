# Material and methods

## Experiments

We reanalysed two pond mesocosm experiments in which the richness and composition of rooted macrophytes, periphyton grazers and invertebrate predators were manipulated simultaneously (Downing and Leibold 2002, Downing 2005). Both experiments were run outdoors at the Kellogg Biological Station (Hickory Corners, Michigan, USA), in 300-L plastic tanks with a sand substrate, filled with well water enriched with NaNO₃ and NaH₂PO₄ to initial concentrations of 1,500 µg N L⁻¹ and 150 µg P L⁻¹, which approximate those of local fishless ponds (Downing 2005, Downing and Leibold 2010). Tanks were inoculated with phytoplankton, periphyton, zooplankton and microbes collected from local ponds, and covered with screen lids that prevented colonization and escape of aquatic species. In both experiments, species were added in a replacement design, which kept the total number of individuals (wet mass for macrophytes) of each functional group constant across treatments (60 g wet mass of macrophytes, 90 grazers and 24 predators), and species compositions were random draws from the species pool of each functional group (Downing and Leibold 2002, Downing 2005). This design changes the density of each species along with richness; however, a separate experiment showed that low and high starting densities (10 versus 50 individuals) of five grazer species did not affect productivity, respiration or decomposition after eight weeks (Downing and Leibold 2002, Supplementary Information). Here, we describe only the aspects of each design that determine our analyses, all of which we reconstructed from the original spreadsheets (Table 1).

The first experiment (hereafter T98; Downing and Leibold 2002, 2010) had a pool of 21 species, seven per functional group. Compositions were stocked with 1, 3 or 5 species of each functional group, so that total stocked richness was 3, 9 or 15 species, and seven compositions were nested within each richness level, which gave 21 compositions replicated in four tanks each (84 tanks). Treatments were established from early April to mid-June, and ecosystem productivity and respiration were measured six times, approximately every two weeks over 12 weeks, beginning four weeks after the communities were complete (Downing and Leibold 2010). Dissolved oxygen was measured 30 min before sunset on two consecutive days and 30 min before the intervening sunrise. Respiration was calculated as the net loss of oxygen per hour between sunset and sunrise, and productivity as the net gain of oxygen per hour between sunrise and sunset; therefore, the latter estimates net community productivity (Downing and Leibold 2010). Phytoplankton contributed an order of magnitude more to these oxygen dynamics than the other functional groups (Downing and Leibold 2002, 2010), so that the manipulated species seem to have affected productivity and respiration mostly indirectly. Decomposition was measured on the same occasions as the daily loss of dry mass of sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*) leaves enclosed in mosquito netting (Downing and Leibold 2002). After the communities had been complete for 40 days, two of the four tanks of each composition received an acid pulse: HCl lowered the pH from about 9.3 to 3.1, and NaOH neutralized the acid 48 h later, whereas control tanks received an equivalent amount of NaCl (about 41 g per tank; Downing and Leibold 2010). In our data, the pulse fell between the second and third of the six sampling periods, which gave 504 observations per response.

The second experiment (hereafter T99; Downing 2005) had a pool of 24 species, eight per functional group, and manipulated functional-group composition in addition to species richness. Accordingly, its 26 species compositions belonged to six design cells defined by the functional groups present and total richness: macrophytes only (M_6), grazers only (G_6), macrophytes and grazers (MG_6), grazers and predators (GP_6), each with three compositions of six species, and all three groups with six (MGP_6) or 18 species (MGP_18), each with seven compositions. Predators were never stocked without grazers, because grazers are their main food in these mesocosms (Downing 2005). Each composition was replicated in four tanks, and a treatment without stocked species was excluded from our analyses. Treatments were completely established by mid-June, and N and P were replenished every two weeks (Downing 2005). Productivity was calculated from dissolved oxygen measured at sunrise and sunset of the same day, and decomposition as the daily loss of dry mass of sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*) leaves enclosed in mosquito netting for approximately 21 days (Downing 2005). In our data, productivity and respiration were measured on 13 occasions over 72 days and decomposition on nine. However, neither the measurement of respiration nor an acid pulse was described by Downing (2005), although the spreadsheets contain both [DETALHE A VERIFICAR com a autora: respiration protocol and acid pulse in T99]. The means of the tanks assigned to the pulse and of the control tanks diverged between the fourth (day 19) and fifth (day 22) sampling occasions, and we used this interval as the pulse date.

## Species compositions

We characterized each composition in two ways. The stocked composition represents the treatment as manipulated. For T98, we parsed it from Table 1 of the Supplementary Information of Downing and Leibold (2002), which distinguishes species stocked and persisting, species stocked and extinct, and species that invaded a treatment; all 21 compositions contained exactly 1, 3 or 5 species per functional group. For T99, we took the stocked composition from the design spreadsheet; however, the species list published with Downing (2005) disagreed with the spreadsheet in two compositions, and in both cases the final biomass data confirmed the spreadsheet. The realized composition represents the community that the treatment produced, and we defined it as the union of species with positive final biomass across the four tanks of each composition. In T98, survivors plus invaders reproduced the published final composition exactly, and four species invaded at least one composition (*Neoplea striola* and *Physa gyrina* in seven compositions each, *Trichocorixa* sp. in three and *Belostoma flumireum* in one). In T99, between zero and five non-stocked species were present at the end of the experiment in each composition. Because every composition had a single value of each phylogenetic metric, all metrics were composition-level predictors.

Our reconstruction also revealed errors in the data sets used in earlier, unpublished analyses of T98. In particular, MPD and MNTD had been attached to the data by recycling a vector of the wrong length, which assigned wrong values to 480 of 504 rows, and Faith's PD had been computed on an earlier tree that underestimated it in nine compositions. Therefore, none of the earlier analyses was reused, and all derived data were rebuilt from the raw files, which we kept read-only.

## Phylogenies and phylogenetic metrics

We assembled pseudochronograms for the species pools of both experiments from node ages available in TimeTree (Kumar et al. 2017), interpolating undated nodes with the bladj algorithm of Phylocom (Webb et al. 2008). For T98, we used a 21-species tree for the stocked compositions and a 19-species tree for the realized compositions, because two macrophytes (*Utricularia vulgaris* and *Vallisneria americana*) went extinct in every tank; for T99, we used a 24-species tree. Species names were updated to current nomenclature when the trees were assembled, so that some differ from those used in the original publications (e.g., *Lithobates catesbeianus* for *Rana catesbeiana*, *Elodea nuttallii* for *E. occidentale* and *Potamogeton zosteriformis* for *P. compressus*). Every tree therefore contained macrophytes, arthropods, molluscs and amphibians, and its deepest splits separated plants from animals and protostomes from vertebrates.

We fixed *a priori* one metric for each of the three dimensions of phylogenetic diversity proposed by Tucker et al. (2017): Faith's phylogenetic diversity (PD, including the root) for richness, mean pairwise distance (MPD) for divergence and the variance of pairwise distances (VPD) for regularity. We also computed mean nearest taxon distance (MNTD) for completeness, but did not use it as a predictor. Metrics were unweighted by abundance and computed with the R package picante (Kembel et al. 2010). Because the branch lengths of a pseudochronogram are partly interpolated, we recomputed all metrics for T99 with Grafen and unit branch lengths. The correlations with the original metrics were at least 0.98 for PD, MPD and VPD under Grafen branch lengths, but decreased to 0.66–0.94 under unit branch lengths (0.74–0.84 for MNTD). Thus, metrics that depend on the deepest splits were robust to the dating, whereas metrics that depend on the shallowest ones were not.

Before modelling, we quantified how much of the variance of each metric was left once the experimental design was accounted for. For that, we regressed each metric on the design strata (richness level in T98 and design cell in T99) and computed the share of its variance within strata. This share is the variance available to estimate an effect of the metric at a fixed design, and it determines how informative the slopes estimated below can be. Additionally, we computed MPD separately within each functional group of the stocked compositions, and tabulated which taxa were responsible for the variation among compositions.

## Statistical models

We fitted Bayesian linear mixed models to the repeated measurements of each response in each experiment. Responses were standardized to zero mean and unit variance, so that priors and effects were on a common scale; respiration in T98 was log-transformed before standardization, whereas productivity and respiration in T99 contained negative values (oxygen-based estimates) and were not transformed. For productivity and respiration we used a Gaussian likelihood, and for decomposition a Student-t likelihood, because posterior predictive checks of Gaussian models showed heavy tails. For tank *j* of composition *c* at sampling period *t*, the linear predictor was

μ_tj = α_s[c] + β z_c + γ_t + δ Pulse_j + θ (Pulse_j × Post_t) + u_c + v_j + ε_tj,

in which α_s[c] is the mean of the design stratum of composition *c* (richness level in T98 and design cell in T99), γ_t the effect of sampling period, δ the difference between pulsed and control tanks before the pulse, θ the effect of the pulse after it, and u_c and v_j normally distributed random intercepts for composition and tank. Residuals within each tank followed a first-order autoregressive process (ε_tj = φ ε_(t−1)j + e_tj), because measurements of the same tank on consecutive occasions are not independent. Additionally, the residual standard deviation of productivity and respiration was modelled as a function of sampling period on the log scale (sum-to-zero contrasts in T99), because grouped posterior predictive checks of models with a constant residual standard deviation showed that it varied up to 1.5-fold among periods in T98 and 3–5-fold in T99, being largest right after the pulse. This structure has an advantage over a single variance term, as it prevents periods with large variance from being treated as outliers. Indeed, a first fit of T99 with Student-t errors absorbed the heteroscedasticity as very heavy tails (ν ≈ 2), and we kept it as a sensitivity analysis.

The phylogenetic predictor z_c entered the model standardized within design strata, that is, as the deviation of the metric of composition *c* from the mean of its stratum divided by the pooled within-stratum standard deviation. As a result, β measures the change in the response, in standard deviations, associated with one standard deviation of phylogenetic diversity among compositions of the same richness (T98) or the same richness and functional-group composition (T99). In T98, we estimated β separately for each richness level and derived the average slope. Each metric (PD, MPD and VPD of the stocked composition, and MPD of the realized composition) was fitted in a separate model. Because we tested three *a priori* metrics per response, we report 95% and 98.33% credible intervals (CI), the latter corresponding to a Bonferroni correction for three tests, and we considered an effect credible only when its 98.33% CI excluded zero.

We then asked whether a single phylogenetic metric could replace the trophic design of T99. For that, we compared a model with design cells, a model with richness only, and three models with richness plus one metric (PD, MPD or VPD) not standardized within cells. For each model, we computed the among-composition variance explained relative to the model with richness only, as 1 − σ²_comp(model)/σ²_comp(richness), over the posterior draws. This quantity measures how much of the variation among compositions is captured by the design or by the metric, after accounting for the random variation among tanks.

To test whether phylogenetic diversity modulated the response to the acid pulse, we split the post-pulse effect into a resistance phase (the first sampling period after the pulse in T98, and the first two in T99) and a recovery phase (the remaining periods), each estimated separately for each richness level. Recovery was then computed as the difference between the two phases. We added the interactions between the standardized metric and each phase, which measure whether compositions with higher phylogenetic diversity than others of the same stratum were less affected by the pulse or recovered faster. This step involved 24 modulation terms per experiment (four metrics, two phases and three responses). Thus, we expected about one 95% CI excluding zero per experiment by chance alone. To check whether any modulation effect depended on single compositions, we refitted the T99 models that showed effects excluding zero, leaving out one composition at a time.

## Priors, computation and model checking

We declared all priors explicitly and checked them with prior predictive simulations (Table 2). Fixed effects received Normal(0, 1) priors, which are weakly informative on the standardized scale because effects of two standard deviations are plausible but larger ones are rare. Standard deviations of random effects received Exponential(2) priors (prior mean of 0.5 standard deviations), the constant residual standard deviation an Exponential(1) prior, the intercept and period effects of the residual-variance submodel Normal(0, 1) priors on the log scale, the autoregressive coefficient a Normal(0, 0.5) prior bounded between −1 and 1, and the degrees of freedom of the Student-t likelihood a Gamma(2, 0.1) prior. Prior predictive simulations were wide for productivity and respiration (99th percentile of absolute simulated values of 16–57 standard deviations), because the residual-variance submodel adds several terms on the log scale. However, power-scaling sensitivity analysis showed that all focal parameters were dominated by the likelihood (Kallioinen et al. 2024). The single flag of potential prior–likelihood conflict concerned the effect of the pulse on productivity in T98, whose sensitivity (0.053) was at the threshold and whose posterior (about −0.45 standard deviations) lay well within its prior.

We ran four chains of 2,000 iterations each, of which 1,000 were warm-up, with a target acceptance rate of 0.95. Models that did not reach a rank-normalized R-hat ≤ 1.01 and bulk and tail effective sample sizes ≥ 400 (Vehtari et al. 2021) were refitted with 4,000 iterations and a target acceptance rate of 0.99. All models reported here met these criteria (maximum R-hat = 1.010, minimum bulk effective sample size = 480) and had no divergent transitions. We evaluated model adequacy with posterior predictive checks that compared the observed mean and standard deviation within each sampling period and design stratum with their posterior predictive distributions (Gabry et al. 2019), because density overlays alone are a weak check for repeated-measures data. We assessed the autoregressive term by comparing models with and without it by approximate leave-one-out cross-validation (Vehtari et al. 2017). The autoregressive coefficient was 0.30 (95% CI: 0.19–0.40) for productivity and 0.52 (0.41–0.62) for respiration in T98, but negligible for decomposition (0.05, −0.03–0.13). Moreover, models of decomposition in T99 with an autoregressive term failed to reproduce the mean of most sampling periods [DETALHE A VERIFICAR: log reports 7 of 10 period groups, but decomposition was measured on nine occasions], whereas models without it reproduced all of them; therefore, we omitted the autoregressive term for decomposition in T99. Finally, period means were reproduced by all final models. However, the standard deviation within some periods was not reproduced for respiration in T99 (eight groups outside the central 90% of the posterior predictive distribution) and, to a lesser degree, for productivity (four) and decomposition (three) in T99 and respiration in T98 (two). None of four alternative variance structures removed this misfit, and the focal slopes did not change among them. We followed the Bayesian analysis reporting guidelines of Kruschke (2021) and the protocol of Zuur and Ieno (2016) to present the models.

## Phylogenetically structured species effects

If each species has an additive effect on a response and species are stocked in equal proportions, the effect of a composition is the mean of the effects of its species. If these species effects covary with phylogenetic relatedness, compositions that share closely related species will have similar responses even when they share no species. We therefore fitted, for T99, a model in which composition effects had covariance σ²_phy P C Pᵀ + σ²_id P Pᵀ, where P is the matrix of species proportions in each composition, C the phylogenetic correlation matrix and σ²_phy and σ²_id the variances of phylogenetically structured and idiosyncratic species effects, plus an independent composition term for non-additive effects (Box 1). This model corresponds to the species-identity part of Diversity–Interactions models (Kirwan et al. 2009) with a phylogenetic prior on species effects, as in phylogenetic mixed models of community data (Ives and Helmus 2011). Additionally, with equal proportions the phylogenetically structured interaction term of Connolly et al. (2011) reduces to a multiple of MPD, which links this model to the MPD models above. The two covariance matrices were projected orthogonally to the richness means and scaled to unit mean diagonal. Because the phylogenetic and identity matrices were strongly aligned in T98, we fitted the model only to T99, and we assessed whether the data were informative about the phylogenetic share, σ²_phy/(σ²_phy + σ²_id), by comparing posterior and prior-only fits. We measured the information gained as the contraction of the 95% interval, 1 − width(posterior)/width(prior), which is zero when the data do not update the prior and one when they fully determine the parameter.

## Design simulation

To identify the kind of experiment that could separate an effect of relatedness from trophic structure and species identity, we simulated experiments under the model of the previous section, adding an effect of MPD within trophic groups (Box 2). We compared five designs with 24, 48 or 96 compositions of four tanks each: (i) random assembly of two or six species per functional group from the T99 pools, as in the original experiments; (ii) compositions of three species per group spread evenly along MPD; (iii) the same gradient chosen to minimize the correlation between the incidence of any species and MPD; (iv) a single focal group (the eight T99 macrophytes) varying while grazers and predators were held constant; and (v) the same with a focal pool of 20 species on simulated pure-birth trees with the crown age of the T99 macrophytes. Species-effect variances (σ_phy and σ_id of 0.15, or 0.21 for one of them and zero for the other) were of the magnitude estimated in the two experiments, and the effect of MPD was zero, 0.1 or 0.2 standard deviations per standard deviation of MPD among random compositions of the species pool. For each of 1,000 replicates per scenario, we estimated the effect of MPD by treating compositions as independent units and by modelling shared species with the two covariance matrices by restricted maximum likelihood, and we refitted a subset (designs ii and v, 24 compositions, no effect of MPD, 100 replicates) in brms with explicit priors to propagate the uncertainty of the variance components.

# Results

## Phylogenetic metrics restate the experimental design

Once the design was accounted for, little variation in the phylogenetic metrics of the stocked compositions remained (Fig. 2). In T98, only 2% of the variance of PD and 11% of the variance of MPD lay within richness levels, whereas 86% of the variance of VPD did. In T99, the share within design cells was even smaller: 1.8% for PD, 2.2% for MPD and 3.8% for VPD. This means that, in both experiments, PD and MPD were almost completely determined by richness and by which functional groups were present. This pattern seems to be driven by the structure of the trees, because every composition combined plants and animals, and the plant–animal and protostome–vertebrate divergences dominated pairwise distances. Interestingly, the realized compositions retained more variation within strata than the stocked ones (T98: 23% for PD and 98% for MPD; T99: 7.7% for PD and 15% for MPD), probably because extinctions and invasions decoupled the realized compositions from the design. However, this variation is itself a response to the treatments, so that it cannot be interpreted as a manipulated predictor.

Within functional groups, MPD varied among compositions only when groups had few species (Appendix S1: Table S1). For instance, the coefficient of variation of within-group MPD among stocked compositions was 0.19–0.31 with three species per group in T98 and 0.19–0.60 with two or three species per group in T99, but 0.04–0.09 with five or six species, because drawing five of seven or six of eight species leaves little room for compositional variation. Additionally, this variation followed a few deep splits that coincided with recognizable taxa: tadpoles versus invertebrates (1,565 Myr) and snails versus crustaceans and insects (1,172 Myr) among grazers, beetles versus true bugs (744 Myr) among predators, and monocots versus *Ceratophyllum*, *Myriophyllum* and *Utricularia* (284–326 Myr) among macrophytes. As a result, MPD within a functional group mostly indexed the presence of tadpoles, snails or beetles in a composition.

## No credible effect of phylogenetic diversity at a fixed design

Phylogenetic diversity did not affect productivity, respiration or decomposition among compositions of the same richness in T98 or of the same design cell in T99 (Fig. 3). In T98, no average slope had a 95% CI excluding zero, and the largest was that of MPD of the stocked composition on decomposition (β = 0.26; 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.57). Moreover, the standard deviation among compositions in models with richness only was small relative to the residual variation (0.15 for productivity, 0.20 for respiration and 0.29 for decomposition). Adding a metric did not reduce it for productivity and respiration, whereas for decomposition the metrics of the stocked composition reduced it to 0.23–0.25, although with slopes whose 95% CIs included zero. In T99, two slopes had 95% CIs excluding zero, but neither had a 98.33% CI that did: MPD of the stocked composition on productivity (β = −0.04; 95% CI: −0.08 to −0.004; 98.33% CI: −0.09 to 0.005) and on decomposition (β = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.002 to 0.29; 98.33% CI: −0.04 to 0.33). Additionally, the slope on decomposition depended on a single composition, and dropped to 0.04 (95% CI: −0.18 to 0.26) when it was left out. Finally, metrics of the realized composition did not affect any response in either experiment.

## Across trophic configurations, the design outperformed any single metric

Across the six design cells of T99, functional-group composition and richness explained 91% (95% CI: 38–100%) of the among-composition variance in productivity and 80% in respiration, although the lower limit of the latter interval was below zero, because the ratio of variances is unbounded below (Fig. 4). Among the phylogenetic metrics, VPD performed best, explaining 70% of the variance in productivity and 66% in respiration (β = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.06–0.16, and β = 0.12, 0.06–0.19, respectively), followed by MPD (24% and 29%). Surprisingly, PD explained almost none of it (2% and 3%). In contrast, neither the design nor any metric explained variation among compositions in decomposition (at most 2%). These intervals were wide because they were estimated from 26 compositions; nonetheless, the design captured more among-composition variation than any single metric in both responses. Therefore, our results suggest that phylogenetic metrics computed over the whole community act as an imperfect summary of trophic structure, rather than as an additional source of information.

## The acid pulse depressed respiration, and phylogenetic diversity did not robustly modulate recovery

The acid pulse reduced respiration by about one standard deviation in both experiments, followed by recovery (Fig. 5). In T98, respiration in pulsed tanks dropped by 0.93–1.14 standard deviations at the first sampling after the pulse, depending on richness, and recovered by 0.42 (95% CI: 0.08–0.77), 0.19 (−0.14–0.52) and 0.71 (0.38–1.05) standard deviations in compositions with 3, 9 and 15 species, respectively. Conversely, productivity was not credibly reduced right after the pulse, but remained 0.42–0.54 standard deviations lower in pulsed tanks during the recovery phase. In T99, respiration dropped by 1.13 (95% CI: 0.90–1.36) and 1.18 (0.85–1.51) standard deviations in compositions with 6 and 18 species, and recovered by 0.90 (0.68–1.14) and 1.13 (0.82–1.44), respectively.

Phylogenetic diversity did not modulate the response to the pulse in T98. Of 24 modulation terms, none had a 95% CI excluding zero, and the strongest was a negative effect of PD on the recovery phase of respiration (−0.17; 95% CI: −0.34 to 0.00). In T99, six terms had 95% CIs excluding zero. Higher MPD of the stocked and realized compositions was associated with a weaker recovery phase of productivity (−0.10, 95% CI: −0.17 to −0.04; and −0.09, −0.15 to −0.02) and respiration (−0.15, −0.24 to −0.05; and −0.13, −0.23 to −0.04). However, these four effects disappeared when a single composition from the GP_6 cell was left out (e.g., −0.04, 95% CI: −0.11 to 0.03, for MPD on productivity). In that composition, the two tanks assigned to the pulse already had lower productivity and respiration than the two control tanks before the pulse, so that much of its apparent recovery seems to reflect a pre-treatment imbalance. In contrast, higher PD of the stocked composition and MPD of the realized composition were associated with a larger increase in decomposition during the recovery phase (0.17, 95% CI: 0.03–0.30; and 0.19, 0.05–0.32), and these effects persisted in 25 of 26 leave-one-out refits at the 95% level. Nonetheless, the same effects were not found in T98, and they were two of 48 modulation terms estimated across experiments. Thus, we treat them as exploratory.

## Phylogenetic and idiosyncratic species effects could not be separated

In the T99 model with phylogenetically structured species effects, the posterior of the phylogenetic share was practically identical to its prior for all responses (contraction of the 95% interval of 0.001–0.003), and the posterior of the non-additive share contracted by 5–7% for productivity and respiration and by 21% for decomposition (Box 1). This means that the data contained almost no information to tell whether the species effects implied by the among-composition variation were structured by phylogeny. Indeed, our design simulation showed that this separation was not achievable in almost any design (Box 2): the 10th–90th percentile range of the estimated phylogenetic share spanned nearly 0–1 in all designs with 24 or 48 compositions, and began to narrow only with a 20-species focal pool and 96 compositions.

Additionally, the simulation showed that treating compositions that share species as independent units inflated false positives, and that this inflation increased with the number of compositions (15–22% with 24 compositions and 26–52% with 96, for a nominal 5%). In contrast, modelling shared species with covariance matrices reduced false positives to 6–8% with 96 compositions, and to 3–5% with 24 compositions when the uncertainty of the variance components was propagated in Bayesian fits. Finally, selecting compositions along MPD with balanced species incidence more than doubled the power to detect an effect of 0.1 standard deviations per standard deviation of MPD relative to random assembly (0.86 versus 0.33 with 24 compositions).
