# Introduction

The recognition that the number and identity of species in a community influence the functioning of ecosystems has catalysed the research of a generation of ecologists, most often through experiments that control species richness and randomize community composition (Bannar-Martin et al. 2018). However, in natural systems, richness and composition change together, because communities are assembled through species losses, species gains and changes in the abundance of resident species. Accordingly, the Community Assembly and the Functioning of Ecosystems (CAFE) perspective argues that it is the entire set of assembly processes that affects ecosystem functioning, not only the part that determines local species richness (Leibold et al. 2017). Under this perspective, which species are present matters at least as much as how many, and changes in richness and composition can act in concert or antagonistically on ecosystem function (Bannar-Martin et al. 2018). As a result, understanding how biodiversity affects ecosystems requires summaries of composition that are informative about function, rather than richness alone.

Phylogenetic diversity has been proposed as one such summary. Because many traits show phylogenetic signal, the amount of evolutionary history represented in a community may estimate its functional trait space, and phylogeny also shapes interactions among species, so that phylogenetic diversity may predict ecosystem functions in communities ranging from single trophic levels to complex networks (Srivastava et al. 2012). According to this view, communities of distantly related species should partition resources more completely and therefore function at higher rates, as found in experiments that assembled plants or bacteria along gradients of relatedness (Cadotte 2013, Venail and Vives 2013), and phylogenetic diversity predicted biodiversity effects on biomass as well as functional diversity did across 29 grassland experiments (Flynn et al. 2011). Conversely, phylogenetic diversity is a surrogate for functional diversity only when the traits that matter are conserved, and its predictive power disappears when trait evolution decouples relatedness from function (Gravel et al. 2012, Tucker et al. 2018). Indeed, maximizing phylogenetic diversity captured less functional diversity than random choice in over one-third of the comparisons among vertebrate clades (Mazel et al. 2018), and a reanalysis of 16 grassland experiments found no effect of phylogenetic diversity on biomass after controlling for richness (Venail et al. 2015, but see Cardinale et al. 2015). Nonetheless, discerning whether evolutionary history adds information about ecosystem functioning beyond richness and species identity remains a challenge, partly because phylogenetic diversity and richness are correlated whenever species are drawn at random from a pool (Cardinale et al. 2015), and partly because branches of equal length on a phylogeny are not necessarily equivalent in their contribution to function (Davies et al. 2016).

This challenge is compounded in food webs, where composition has a vertical dimension. Theory predicts that food-web structure can change the strength, direction and shape of biodiversity–ecosystem functioning relationships relative to single trophic levels (Thébault and Loreau 2003), and that ecosystem processes in multitrophic communities depend on how species and their abundances are distributed across trophic levels (Duffy et al. 2007, Wang and Brose 2018). From a CAFE perspective, assembly in food webs involves extinctions and colonizations at several trophic levels at once, so that the composition of each level is reshaped by the others. In such communities, a phylogeny could seem a better proxy than measured traits, because the traits that determine how a macrophyte, a grazer or a predator affects an ecosystem are not comparable across trophic levels, whereas closely related species within a trophic level may share unmeasured effect traits. However, phylogenetic diversity computed over a whole food web combines plants, molluscs, arthropods and vertebrates, and its value may be dominated by the deep divergences among trophic groups rather than by relatedness within them. Therefore, in multitrophic communities, phylogenetic diversity may restate trophic structure instead of adding information about it, and the phylogenetic signal that is present in a regional phylogeny may not exist at the scale of an experimental community (Srivastava et al. 2012).

Pond mesocosms in which macrophytes, periphyton grazers and invertebrate predators were assembled simultaneously offer an opportunity to examine this problem. In two such experiments, Downing and Leibold (2002) and Downing (2005) manipulated species richness and composition across three trophic levels, and found that the effects of composition on productivity, respiration and decomposition were as large as or larger than those of richness. Additionally, half of the tanks received an acid pulse, and respiration was more resilient in species-rich communities, although composition did not affect resistance or resilience (Downing and Leibold 2010). The species pools of these experiments included rooted plants, molluscs, crustaceans, insects and amphibians, and the communities underwent extinctions and invasions during the experiments, so that the stocked and realized compositions differed. As a result, these experiments allow us to ask whether relatedness, a candidate summary of composition, explains the composition effects documented for these food webs, both in the communities as manipulated and in the communities that assembled from them.

However, experiments that manipulate diversity at more than one trophic level are still rare (Douglass et al. 2008, Lefcheck et al. 2015), and the consequences of phylogenetic diversity for functioning in multitrophic systems remain poorly studied (Eisenhauer et al. 2019). The studies that brought phylogeny into multitrophic settings either manipulated it at a single trophic level and followed its effects on consumers (Alavez et al. 2023) or related it to ecosystem functions in natural communities (Thompson et al. 2015, Staab et al. 2021). Thus, to the best of our knowledge, the hypothesis that phylogenetic diversity predicts ecosystem functioning in complex food webs (Srivastava et al. 2012) has not been tested in experiments in which three trophic levels were manipulated at once. Moreover, because compositions drawn from the same species pool share species, analyses that treat compositions as independent units may overstate the evidence for any composition-level predictor (Tsang and Cadotte 2025), which has not been considered in previous analyses of these experiments.

Here, we reanalysed the two pond mesocosm experiments of Downing and Leibold (2002) and Downing (2005) to test whether phylogenetic diversity explains ecosystem functioning in multitrophic communities. Specifically, we asked: (i) how much of the variation in phylogenetic diversity among compositions is independent of richness and trophic structure; (ii) whether phylogenetic diversity affects productivity, respiration and decomposition among compositions with the same richness and trophic structure; (iii) whether a single phylogenetic metric can replace explicit functional-group composition in explaining variation among trophic configurations; (iv) whether phylogenetic diversity modulates the resistance and recovery of these processes after an acid pulse; and (v) whether phylogenetically structured species effects can be separated from idiosyncratic species effects, and which experimental design would allow it. We used the richness, divergence and regularity dimensions of phylogenetic diversity (Tucker et al. 2017), computed for the stocked and realized compositions. Following Srivastava et al. (2012), we expected that, if relatedness within trophic groups captures complementarity in unmeasured effect traits, phylogenetic diversity would increase ecosystem rates and their recovery after disturbance among compositions with the same richness and trophic structure.
